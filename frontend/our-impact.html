Keep tourism income local

Adventure tourism in Nepal can be structured so that most of what a traveller pays leaves the country again — an international operator, an international guide, an imported supply chain, and a local economy that sees the price of a bed and nothing else. The alternative is not complicated, it is just a set of choices about who gets paid.

The choices that are structural here: the guides and support team are Nepali and employed directly. Accommodation on the hill routes is village lodges and teahouses rather than operator-owned camps. Transport, food and permits are bought in-country.

What is not yet published: what proportion of trip revenue is actually spent in Nepal, what the guides and support team are paid, and how many of the lodges used on each route are locally owned. All three are recorded per departure in the admin panel and all three need High Route’s own figures. Until they are in, the structure above is a description of how trips are run, not a claim about amounts.

Protect the places we go

Group size is the first lever and the least glamorous one. Trips here cap between 8 and 12 depending on the route, and on the remote circuits lower than that — not as a luxury positioning but because a village of forty people and a single teahouse cannot absorb a group of twenty without the experience changing for everyone who lives there.

The second is waste. Above the roadhead there is no waste collection: whatever is carried up is either carried down or left. That makes packaging an operational decision rather than an environmental gesture, and it is why bottled water is the thing to get right first — a twelve-day trip with twelve people buying three bottles a day is several hundred plastic bottles in a valley with nowhere to put them.

What is not yet published: litres of bottled water avoided, and what is contributed to trail maintenance and to the conservation areas each route crosses. The fields exist per departure; the figures have to be counted on trip rather than estimated afterwards.

Travel with communities, not through them

The difference is whether a village is a place where a group stops and spends money, or a place a group rides past and photographs. Most of what makes the difference is unglamorous: asking before entering a gompa, not riding through a threshing floor, buying lunch where you stop rather than carrying it past, and taking the time to be a guest rather than a passing vehicle.

Upper Mustang and Manaslu are restricted areas with their own cultural protocols, and in both the guide is also an interpreter of what is and is not acceptable. That is not a bonus feature of a licensed guide requirement — it is the point of it.

What is not yet published: the community partners worked with, by name, and direct contributions to community projects. A count of "partners" without the names would be a number pretending to be a relationship, so neither appears until the names can.

Reduce what can be reduced

What is genuinely reducible on a Nepal trip: domestic flights where a road journey works, vehicle kilometres through route planning, single-use packaging, and generator-charged power. What is not: the international flight that got you here, which is almost certainly the largest single part of the footprint of your trip and is not within an operator’s gift to change.

Road and air kilometres per traveller is on the list of figures to publish precisely because it does not flatter anyone. An operator publishing only the numbers that look good is doing marketing.

What cannot be eliminated

A long-haul flight to Kathmandu is the dominant environmental cost of a Himalayan adventure holiday and nothing on this page offsets it. We are not going to tell you otherwise, sell you a carbon-neutral label, or describe a trip to the other side of the world as sustainable.

What can honestly be said is narrower: once you are here, the money can stay here, the group can be small enough not to overwhelm the places it passes through, and the waste can leave with it. That is a real difference between operators, and it is worth asking about. It is not the same thing as a trip having no impact.

How to hold us to this. Every claim on this page is either a description of how trips are structured — which you can check by asking who guides, where you sleep, and how big the group is — or a figure, in which case it is either published with a source or shown as not yet published. If you find a third category anywhere on this site, that is a mistake and we would like to know.
