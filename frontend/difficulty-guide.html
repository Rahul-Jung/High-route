How we grade trips

Difficulty Guide

One scale from 1 to 5 across every activity, so that Moderate means the same amount of commitment on a trek as on a ride — plus separate scores for the three things that actually make a trip hard, because they are not the same thing and a single number hides which one will stop you.

The five levels

Why three scores, not one

A single difficulty number tells you how hard a trip is but not why, and the why is the part that decides whether you should book it. Three examples from this site, all of which would collapse into the same middle of a one-number scale:

  • A high-altitude crossing can be gentle underfoot and still turn you back at 5,000 m. The limit is acclimatisation, and no amount of training in a gym changes it.
  • A low forest trail can be technically serious at 2,000 m, where altitude is irrelevant and bike handling decides everything.
  • A long jeep-road descent can be neither technical nor high and still break people, because it is six hours a day for eight days running.

So every adventure carries an overall level and a score out of five for each dimension its activity is graded on. If you know which of the three is your weak point, you can read the scores and make the decision yourself instead of guessing from a label.

How it differs by activity

The dimensions are not interchangeable between activities, so they are declared per activity rather than assumed to be the same three. Rafting is graded on swimming ability; trekking on what is underfoot rather than on bike handling. Activities not yet on the site are listed too, with the scores they would use.

Where we are honest about not knowing

This catalogue started as mountain-bike-only, with a single difficulty number per trip. When it became a universal model, the three dimensions had to come from somewhere — and the only honest answer was to seed all three from the existing overall level and flag them as unassessed, rather than invent a spread that nobody had actually judged.

So on some trips you will see a note saying the three scores have not been assessed separately yet, and that they currently mirror the overall level. That note is accurate. It will disappear from a trip when somebody who has ridden or walked it sets the three scores properly. A trip without the note has had them set.

Fitness is also the one dimension you can genuinely prepare for. Altitude is not, and technical skill is not in the six weeks before a trip. If you are between two trips, the altitude score is the one to take seriously.
