Start here

Most of what an operator’s safety page contains is unverifiable by the reader. Certifications, evacuation arrangements, medical protocols, insurance cover — you cannot check any of it from a web page, which is precisely why it is so easy to write.

So this page splits in two. Everything down to "Emergencies" describes the risks themselves and how trips are structured around them, which is checkable: ask us, and ask any other operator you are considering the same questions. Everything under "What is not documented yet" is the part High Route has to supply before it can honestly appear here, and it is listed rather than quietly omitted.

The real risks

In rough order of how often they actually cause a problem on a Himalayan trip, rather than in order of how dramatic they sound:

  • Altitude illness. The most common reason people cut a trip short, and the least related to fitness.
  • Stomach illness. Unglamorous and very common. It ruins more days than anything on this list.
  • Road travel. Statistically the most dangerous part of a trip to Nepal, and the part nobody worries about. Mountain roads, long days, and other drivers.
  • Falls and collisions on the bike. Mostly minor, occasionally not, and more likely on day six than day one — fatigue, not terrain, is usually the factor.
  • Cold and exposure on high passes, where a clear morning and an afternoon storm can be thirty degrees apart.
  • Remoteness. Not a risk in itself, but it multiplies all of the above: the same injury is a different event two days from a road.

Altitude

Above about 2,500 m, altitude illness becomes possible; above 3,500 m it becomes common; above 4,500 m it has to be actively managed. Fitness does not protect you, youth does not protect you, and having been fine at altitude before does not guarantee you will be fine this time.

What this means in practice for the trips here: itineraries that go high include acclimatisation days, and those days are not optional extras to be traded for a shorter trip. The only reliable treatment for altitude illness is to go down, and an itinerary with no slack in it makes the decision to go down more expensive than it should be.

Every adventure page states its maximum altitude and carries an altitude score out of five. If you have not been above 3,000 m before, start with a trip scored 2 or below on altitude — the Kathmandu Valley and Pokhara trips are there for that reason.

Group size and guides

Group sizes are capped per trip and shown on every adventure page. The safety reason, as distinct from the experience reason, is simple: a guide can only watch so many people on exposed ground, and if somebody has to go down, somebody has to go with them — which is only possible if the group is small enough that the rest are not left unguided.

Guides are Nepali, from the regions they guide in, and carry a first-aid kit and a communication device. The Guides page lists each one’s experience and languages, and only the qualifications that have actually been verified.

Weather and changed plans

Snow closing a pass, a landslide across a road section, a cancelled mountain flight: all ordinary, all planned for, and all reasons an itinerary changes on the ground. The guide makes that call.

You will not be taken over a high pass in bad conditions to keep a schedule. If that costs you a summit day or a section of the route, that is the correct trade and the itinerary has alternatives built in for it.

Insurance — a condition of booking

Travel insurance is required, not recommended, on every trip on this site. It must cover:

  • The activity you are doing — many policies exclude mountain biking entirely, or exclude it off-road.
  • The maximum altitude of your trip. This is the clause that catches people out. A great many standard policies stop at 3,000 m or 4,000 m, which excludes most of what is on this site.
  • Helicopter evacuation and repatriation. In the Nepali mountains, evacuation means a helicopter, and a helicopter means a bill in the tens of thousands.
  • Trip cancellation, which also covers you if a departure is cancelled and you have booked flights separately.

Read the altitude clause specifically, in the policy wording rather than the summary. "Trekking" being covered does not mean trekking at 5,400 m is covered.

Emergencies

What is in place: guides carry a first-aid kit and a means of communication, group sizes allow somebody to descend with an ill traveller, and itineraries in remote areas are planned around where help can and cannot reach.

What a trip page tells you honestly: Manaslu’s page says there is no road access and no quick way out of the middle of that circuit, and that evacuation means a helicopter and weather that allows one. That is the kind of statement a safety page should contain, and it belongs on the trip it applies to.

Route maps, GPS and equipment

Route maps are for planning, not navigation. Where a trip's itinerary has recorded overnight-stop positions, they are shown on that trip's map. They are approximate positions, not surveyed GPX data — and a trip with no recorded positions shows no map rather than an invented one.

The live GPS tracker on this site is a demonstration of how trip tracking could work. It is not connected to a real operations desk or emergency service, and it is not a substitute for a satellite emergency communicator. Do not rely on it for anyone's safety.

Equipment. Each trip page lists the essential and the recommended equipment for that trip where we have published it. Essential means every traveller is expected to have it; recommended means it genuinely improves comfort or safety but is not required. If a trip includes a support vehicle or a mechanic, it is listed in that trip's inclusions — if it is not listed, assume it is not included and ask.

What is not documented yet

The following have not been supplied by High Route and therefore do not appear anywhere on this site. They are listed here so that their absence is visible rather than something you have to notice.

  • Written emergency and evacuation procedure, per region.
  • Which guides hold which first-aid or wilderness medical qualification, and when each was last renewed. The Guides page shows only what has been verified, which today is a short list.
  • The operator’s own liability insurance and its limits.
  • Any safety accreditation or external audit.
  • A 24-hour emergency contact number for the duration of a trip, and who answers it.
  • The named medical facilities and evacuation providers relied on in each region.

If any of these matters to your decision — and the last two should — ask us directly and get the answer in writing before you pay a deposit. That is better advice than anything we could put on this page today.
