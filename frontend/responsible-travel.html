Water and plastic

The single highest-impact thing a traveller can change on a Nepal trip. Above the roadhead there is no waste collection: a plastic bottle bought at 3,500 m either gets carried back down or stays in the valley. A twelve-day trip with twelve people buying three bottles a day is several hundred bottles.

Bring a bottle you can refill and a way to treat water — a steriliser pen, chlorine dioxide drops or a filter. All three work; the drops are the cheapest and the least likely to break. Lodges sell boiled water by the litre, which costs more than a bottle and is worth it.

Where your money goes

Most of the money a traveller spends in Nepal beyond the trip price is spent in the week around it, and where it is spent makes a real difference:

  • Buy lunch where you stop rather than carrying it past. A teahouse makes its margin on food, not on beds.
  • Pay for the hot shower and the charging. It is generator fuel or solar capacity somebody paid for.
  • Buy things made here. A great deal of what is sold as Nepali handicraft in Thamel is imported. Ask.
  • Tip the support team directly where that is the convention, and ask your guide what the local norm actually is rather than guessing from a guidebook written a decade ago.

Cultural ground rules

Not an exhaustive list, and your guide is the authority on the specific place — but these come up on every trip:

  • Walk clockwise around stupas, mani walls and chortens, and pass them on the left.
  • Take your shoes off before entering a gompa or a home, and ask before entering either.
  • Do not touch anyone’s head, and do not point the soles of your feet at a person or a shrine.
  • Use your right hand to give and receive, or both hands for something formal.
  • Dress covered — shoulders and knees — in villages and at religious sites. Riding kit is fine on the trail and is not fine walking into a monastery.
  • Public affection between couples reads differently here than in Europe. Keep it low-key.

Photographs

Ask first, every time, and accept no as an answer without negotiating. This applies especially to monks, to older people, to anyone working, and to children — and it applies even when the shot is very good.

Photography is restricted or forbidden inside many gompas, sometimes for conservation reasons (flash and old pigment) and sometimes because it is a place of worship rather than a monument. Your guide will know. Do not photograph a cremation at Pashupatinath.

Trail etiquette

Riders are the newest users of most of these trails and have the least claim on them:

  • Yield to everything. Walkers, porters carrying loads, pack animals, locals going about their day. A porter under 30 kg cannot step aside for you.
  • Stop for pack trains and stand on the uphill side of the trail. A spooked mule on an exposed section is dangerous for everyone, and most of all for the mule.
  • Do not skid turns or cut switchbacks. These are working paths that somebody maintains by hand, and a braking rut becomes an erosion channel in the next monsoon.
  • Slow right down through villages and around threshing floors, drying crops and children.
  • Carry your rubbish out, including the energy-bar wrappers and the inner tube.

Giving, and why sweets are a bad idea

Handing out sweets, pens and money to children is well-intentioned and does measurable harm: it teaches that approaching foreigners is more productive than school, and in villages with no dentist, sweets are not a kindness.

If you want to contribute, give to a school, a health post or a trail-maintenance effort through your guide, who knows which ones are real. Giving to an institution rather than to a child is less satisfying and considerably more useful.

If your trip uses porters

Portering is skilled work and a legitimate living, and the problem is never that it exists — it is when it is done badly. What to look for, and what to ask an operator before you book anything in Nepal:

  • A weight limit per porter, and whether it is enforced.
  • Whether porters have boots, warm clothing and sunglasses appropriate to the altitude they are being taken to, or whether they are expected to provide their own for a 5,000 m pass.
  • Whether they are insured, and for what.
  • Where they sleep and what they eat — on some trips it is not the lodge.

High Route’s own porter policy — weight limits, equipment, insurance and accommodation — has not been supplied and so is not described here. On trips that use porters, the Porter support field on the trip page is where it will appear. If it is blank on a trip you are considering, that is a question worth asking before you book.

What this does not fix

Everything on this page is worth doing and none of it offsets the flight that brought you here, which is almost certainly the largest environmental cost of your trip. We are not going to pretend otherwise, and the Our Impact page says the same thing at more length.

What these choices genuinely affect is the share of your money that stays in the valley you rode through, the amount of plastic left above the roadhead, and whether the people who live on these trails are glad to see the next group. Those are not small things. They are just not the same thing as a trip being harmless.
